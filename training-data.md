## A Note Before You Start

This text is the prequel to a longer one about dopamine, neurodivergence, pool tables, and prediction engines. You don't need to read that one first. But if you've already read it, this is the text that was always quietly underneath it.

It is about something most people never examine directly — something so close to the face that the average person can no more see it than a fish can see water.

**The stories you tell yourself.**

Not bedtime stories. Not the plot of your favorite show. Not a metaphor. The actual, running, second-by-second internal narrative that decides what you notice, what you avoid, what you pursue, how hard you try, when you quit, who you think you are, and what you believe is possible for you.

That narrative is not decoration. It is not personality. It is not some soft, therapist-speak concept that can be safely ignored by anyone who considers themselves rational.

It is the **operating system** beneath your behavior. And most people are running code they never wrote, never reviewed, and never consented to.

This text has three jobs:

1. Show you what stories actually *are*, mechanically — not philosophically
2. Trace where yours came from, and why they are almost certainly not entirely your own
3. Explain why updating them is the highest-leverage thing a human being can do — and what that actually looks like

If two paragraphs feel uncomfortably personal, you are reading the right text. If you finish it and start watching your own internal monologue differently, good. That's the point.

## We Are Not Homo Sapiens First

The standard story is that humans are the rational animal. The thinking animal. The one that reasons its way through decisions, weighs evidence, calculates outcomes, and arrives at correct conclusions.

This is a very flattering story. It also happens to be wrong.

What the last fifty years of cognitive science, behavioral economics, evolutionary psychology, and neuroscience converge on is something slightly less comfortable:

> **We are, first and foremost, the story-making animal.**

Homo narrans. The narrating human.

Yuval Noah Harari made this culturally famous in *Sapiens*, but the scientific scaffolding is older and deeper. Roger Schank and Robert Abelson's 1977 work on *scripts* — the mental templates humans use to navigate social situations — showed that human cognition organizes around narrative structure almost reflexively.[^schank] Daniel Kahneman's decades of work on "System 1" thinking — fast, automatic, emotionally-driven — demonstrated that most human decisions happen before the rational narrative even boots.[^kahneman] Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis showed that even the most "logical" decisions are silently guided by emotional memory, not pure analysis.[^damasio]

We don't reason our way to a conclusion and then experience a feeling about it. We experience a feeling — shaped entirely by the stories encoded in our nervous system — and then *reason backward* to construct a justification we call a decision.

The rationality is real. But it is almost never first. The story is first.

And the story was there long before you were old enough to interrogate it.

## What a Story Actually Is

Let's be precise. Not poetic — precise.

A "story," in the neuroscientific sense, is a **predictive model** stored in the nervous system as a pattern of expected relationships between things.

Every experience you have ever had — every success, every failure, every embarrassment, every moment of joy, every rejection, every unexpected reward — left a trace in the nervous system in the form of a compressed pattern: *"When X, then usually Y."* When I try something hard, I usually fail. When I put myself out there, people usually pull back. When I focus too long on one thing, people get frustrated with me. When I follow my curiosity, something interesting usually happens. When I speak up in a room, people usually listen.

These patterns are not just memories. They are **priors** — the predictive weightings the nervous system uses to navigate the next moment. The brain doesn't experience life and then remember it. The brain *predicts* life, checks the prediction against what actually happened, and updates the weights accordingly.

Every neuroscientist who works on predictive coding — Karl Friston is the most prominent — will tell you the same thing in slightly different language: the brain is a prediction machine, constantly generating an internal model of the world, constantly trying to minimize the difference between what it expected and what it got.[^friston]

The stories you tell yourself are not just narratives. They are literally the **internal model**. They are the simulation your brain is running underneath everything else. And the simulation is always on. It runs when you're solving a problem, when you're choosing what to order, when you're deciding whether to apply for the job, when you're reading someone's face across a table.

Here is the part that matters:

> **The quality of the simulation determines the quality of the life.**

Not intelligence. Not talent. Not resources. The fidelity, flexibility, and honesty of the internal model.

A person running a simulation that says *"effort pays off and failure is information"* navigates the same objective world completely differently than a person running a simulation that says *"effort is pointless and failure is who I am."*

Same world. Same options. Two entirely different outcomes — because the story shapes what possibilities the nervous system even allows through for consideration.

## Where Your Stories Came From

Here is the unsettling part.

Most of the stories you are running right now — the ones that decide what you pursue, who you trust, what you think you deserve, what you think is possible — were **not written by you.**

They were written:

- By a parent who was doing their best on incomplete information
- By a school system designed for a particular kind of mind that may not have been yours
- By peers who were themselves running stories they hadn't examined
- By a culture that had economic, political, and social incentives embedded in every message it sent
- By early experiences that happened before you had the cognitive development to contextualize them
- By biology — the particular wiring you showed up with, which shapes what registers as signal and what registers as noise

The technical term for this — borrowed from machine learning — is **training distribution**.

An AI model is only as good as the data it was trained on. If the training data was biased, incomplete, skewed, or unrepresentative, the model will produce systematically biased, incomplete, skewed, and unrepresentative outputs — even if it is technically sophisticated. The model has no way to know what it was never shown.

Neither do you.

A child raised in a family where love was conditional on performance gets handed a very specific training distribution: *attention = performance = worth*. That child grows into an adult who is inexplicably driven, chronically anxious, and quietly convinced that they are one mistake away from being fundamentally unlovable — and has no idea why.

A child raised in a community where risk-taking was punished and conformity was rewarded gets handed a different training distribution: *safety = compliance = survival*. That adult struggles to start the company, take the creative leap, say the difficult truth — and frames it to themselves as "just being practical."

A child who was told, in a hundred small ways, that they were "too much" — too loud, too intense, too curious, too restless, too scattered — gets handed a distribution that says: *"your natural way of being is a problem."* That adult spends twenty years apologizing for the exact qualities that are their greatest strengths.

None of these training distributions were handed to the child maliciously. Most were handed by people who were themselves running the story they had been handed. The distributions pass forward the way accents do — not as deliberate instruction but as the texture of ordinary experience, absorbed before the ability to question it exists.

> **The priors were inherited. The next update is yours.**

But the update can only happen once you can actually see the prior. And that is surprisingly hard — because the most powerful stories are the ones that don't feel like stories at all. They feel like *reality*.

## The Confirmation Engine

The reason most people never update their core stories has nothing to do with intelligence or willpower.

It is architecture.

The human brain is not designed to be a neutral evidence-evaluator. It is designed to maintain a stable, predictive model of the world — because a stable model is computationally cheaper to run than constant re-evaluation. And the most efficient way to maintain a stable model is to **preferentially process evidence that confirms it and discount evidence that contradicts it**.

This is the neural basis of confirmation bias. It is not a bug. It is an extremely efficient feature for an organism that needs to make thousands of predictions per day without burning excessive energy.

But it has a critical side effect: **stories are self-sealing**.

A person running the story *"I always fail when things get hard"* will:
- Notice each failure more vividly than each success
- Attribute successes to luck, context, or external factors
- Attribute failures to the self
- Disengage slightly earlier each time (to avoid the painful failure)
- Receive fewer successes as a result of the earlier disengagement
- Interpret that reduced success rate as confirmation of the original story

The story creates the conditions that prove itself. The prediction shapes the behavior that fulfills the prediction.

Psychologists call this a **self-fulfilling prophecy**. Cognitive behavioral therapists call it a **cognitive distortion schema**. Statisticians call it **distribution shift** — where the model's behavior changes the data it receives, which reinforces the model, in a closed loop.

Whatever you call it, the mechanism is the same: *the story running in your head is quietly engineering the evidence that keeps it running*.

This is why no amount of external success, praise, or rational counter-evidence ever seems to fully dislodge a core limiting narrative. A thousand people can tell you you're brilliant and the quiet voice can still say *"they don't really know me."* The voice is not irrational. It is operating exactly as designed — filtering for the data that keeps the internal model stable.

> The only thing that actually updates a deeply held story is not argument. It is **repeated, emotionally-charged, incompatible experience** — combined with a conscious decision to let the new data actually land.

That is what therapy is. That is what coaching is. That is what a transformative relationship is. That is, in a very real sense, what growth is: the slow, deliberate accumulation of incompatible evidence until the old story is no longer the cheapest model to maintain.

## The Shape of a Limiting Story

Most limiting stories don't present themselves as "I believe X." They present themselves as observation.

They hide inside phrases like *"the way things are"* and *"just being realistic"* and *"that's just how I am."*

Here are the most common ones. Some of these will feel true. Most of them are not.

**On effort:**
> *"If I were smart enough, it wouldn't be this hard."*
> — Wrong. Difficulty is not evidence of deficit. Difficulty is the condition of growth.

**On failure:**
> *"Failing means I wasn't good enough."*
> — Wrong. Failing means the prediction was incorrect. Update the model and run the next experiment.

**On visibility:**
> *"If I put myself fully out there and it doesn't land, I'll be destroyed."*
> — Wrong. Rejection is data about fit, audience, timing, or presentation. It is rarely data about worth.

**On time:**
> *"I missed my window. It's too late for me."*
> — Almost always wrong. Almost always a story built on watching someone else's trajectory and applying it to a different life.

**On nature:**
> *"This is just how I'm wired. I can't change this."*
> — Partially true (biology is real), mostly wrong (the nervous system updates until death — see: neuroplasticity).

**On worthiness:**
> *"People like me don't get to have things like that."*
> — Not a fact. A prior installed so early it wears the costume of bedrock.

The reason these stories are so persistent is not that they are correct. It is that they were **installed at high emotional intensity, in repeated doses, during developmental periods when the nervous system was literally building its first model of what to expect from the world.** They have the texture of permanence because they are old. Old does not mean accurate.

## Stories as Strategy

Here is something that goes mostly unnoticed:

**Your internal narrative is not just a description of what is. It is a prescription for how to behave.**

The story *"I always fail when things get hard"* doesn't just predict failure. It prescribes early withdrawal as the rational strategy. Why invest fully in something that will ultimately confirm the story? Partial investment protects partial ego. The story creates the strategy that produces the outcome.

The story *"people who succeed are just lucky"* doesn't just describe success. It prescribes passivity as the rational response. Why build the habit, do the reps, take the risk? The mechanism doesn't respond to effort anyway. The story makes inaction logical.

The story *"I'm not the kind of person who does X"* doesn't just describe identity. It prescribes avoidance of X as identity-consistency. Trying X would create dissonance — because it contradicts the established self-model. Better not to try. The story makes the attempt feel wrong before it starts.

This is why narrative change is **strategic**, not cosmetic. Swapping one story for another does not just change how you feel. It changes:

- What goals you allow yourself to set
- What approaches you consider available to you
- What evidence you weight as relevant
- What obstacles feel fatal versus surmountable
- What kind of help you're willing to ask for
- What you think you deserve to have on the other side

The story is the strategy. Which means **the most important strategic work any person can do is not to optimize their tactics inside the existing narrative but to examine whether the narrative itself is serving them**.

Most people never do this. Most people spend an entire career working harder and harder inside a story that is quietly determining that the harder work cannot pay off in the direction they actually want to go.

## The Training Distribution Problem

Let's get technical for a moment. It will pay off.

When researchers train a machine learning model on a dataset, they worry intensely about one specific failure mode: the model learning to perform well *on the training distribution* but failing to generalize to the real world.

This happens when the training data is:
- **Biased** — systematically skewed toward certain examples
- **Incomplete** — missing entire categories of important cases
- **Unrepresentative** — drawn from a population that doesn't match the deployment environment
- **Historically contingent** — accurate for the era it was collected in, but outdated now

A model trained only on data from one city will fail in others. A model trained on data from ten years ago may fail on present conditions. A model trained to optimize for the wrong outcome will be brilliant at the wrong thing.

Sound familiar?

Your internal narrative model was trained on a dataset that was:

- **Biased** — by the family you happened to be born into
- **Incomplete** — by the experiences you happened to have (or not have) by age twenty
- **Unrepresentative** — of the full range of what people actually do, achieve, become
- **Historically contingent** — accurate for the environment you grew up in, potentially mismatched to where you actually live now

The failure modes are the same. A person who grew up in a high-scarcity environment may run a scarcity model in a context of genuine abundance — and experience genuine suffering from a misaligned prior. A person trained in a social environment where vulnerability was punished may run a closed-off model in relationships that would reward openness — and wonder why intimacy is always slightly out of reach.

The model is not broken. **The model is operating exactly as trained. The training distribution just wasn't the whole world.**

The correction is not self-criticism. It is the same thing ML engineers do when they catch distribution shift: **deliberately expose the model to new, high-quality training examples, monitor the outputs closely, and update incrementally**.

Therapy is deliberate retraining. Coaching is deliberate retraining. Deep relationships are deliberate retraining. Reading texts like this one is the very beginning of deliberate retraining — the moment you start to notice that you have a model at all.

## Stories Are the Bias in the Room

There is a subtler form of this that is worth naming.

Stories are not just running in individual heads. They are running in rooms. In teams. In organizations. In cultures. In nations.

Every organization has a founding story — an origin narrative about what it is, what it's for, and who it serves. That story is not decorative. It determines:

- Which opportunities the organization notices versus ignores
- Which failures it learns from versus excuses
- Which people it promotes versus sidelines
- Which problems it treats as tractable versus inevitable

Every family has a story — unspoken but load-bearing — about what success looks like, what emotions are allowable, what kind of help it's okay to need.

Every culture has a story about which minds matter, which bodies are correct, which ways of learning are legitimate, which ways of being are disorders to be corrected rather than differences to be understood.

The reason "a rising tide lifts all boats" is sometimes empirically false is that the boats without access to the dominant narrative can't dock where the tide is rising. The story decides who gets to benefit from the conditions.

This is not a political argument. It is a structural observation. The story is not decoration. **The story determines the physics of the room.**

And the individual sitting in that room is not running a story in isolation. They are running a story that is continuously being reinforced, rewarded, or suppressed by the stories in the systems around them. Changing a personal narrative in an unchanged environment is swimming against a current. Possible. But accurately named.

## The Rewrite

So how does it actually work? How do you update a story that has been running for decades, that has the full weight of the confirmation engine behind it, that feels not like a belief but like the texture of reality?

Slowly. Deliberately. And almost never through argument.

Here is what the neuroscience and clinical research actually support:

**1. Naming the story.**
You cannot update what you cannot see. The first step is almost always the same: *"I notice I'm running a story right now. What is it? Say it plainly."* Not psychoanalysis — just honest labeling. *"I'm telling myself that asking for help means I'm weak."* The act of making the story explicit moves it from automatic to visible. From background process to foreground object. From weather to window.

**2. Questioning the source.**
*"Where did this come from? Who taught me this? Was that person right? Was that environment representative?"* Not to blame — blame is just another story. To contextualize. The story was useful once, in a different environment. It might not belong here.

**3. Actively collecting counter-evidence.**
The confirmation engine will not do this automatically. You have to deliberately seek it. Find the people, examples, data points, and experiences that contradict the limiting story — and slow down enough to let them register. This is not toxic positivity. It is deliberate recalibration of a skewed dataset.

**4. Acting from the new story before it feels true.**
This is the uncomfortable part. The new story — *"failure is information"*, *"I deserve to take up space"*, *"my way of thinking is a strength"* — will feel false at first. Because it is new, and new paths through the neural forest are thin and uncertain. Act from it anyway. Each action reinforces the path. After enough repetitions, the path begins to feel more solid than the old one.

**5. Environment.**
This one is underrated. The stories running in the room are always exerting pressure on the stories running in the head. If every person around you reinforces the limiting narrative, updating the narrative requires extraordinary effort. If even a few people in your environment reflect the new story back to you, the update becomes possible. **Who is in the room is also strategic.**

> You cannot think your way out of a story. You have to act your way out of it, slowly, through the body, in environments that make the new story plausible.

This is the sentence that takes most people the longest to accept. Because the mind wants a cognitive solution — a good-enough argument that makes the story collapse. That almost never works. The story is not an argument. It is a groove in the nervous system. And grooves are deepened or recut through repetition, experience, and embodied action — not through persuasion.

## Before the Story, the Story

There is one more layer to this.

The stories you tell yourself about your tasks, your relationships, your career — those are the surface layer. They are updateable through the methods above, and updating them matters enormously.

But underneath those stories is a deeper layer. A story about the *nature of stories themselves*.

Some people believe: *"My internal narrative is just how I see things. It accurately reflects reality."*

Others believe: *"My internal narrative is a model I'm running — useful, imperfect, updateable, and not the same thing as the world."*

The first belief makes change nearly impossible. The second makes it merely hard.

Everything in this text is pointing at one shift: from the first belief to the second. From *"this is how things are"* to *"this is the story I am currently running about how things are."* That single shift in meta-cognition — noticing that the narrator exists and is not infallible — is the entire game.

The pool table knows no stories. Newton's laws apply regardless of what you believe about yourself. The ball drops or it doesn't. And every time a player sits with the honest, immediate verdict of physics — without defending the miss or hiding from it — they practice something that transfers far beyond the table:

> **The willingness to have the model corrected.**

That willingness is rare. It is also the single most important cognitive skill a human being can develop. Because the model is always imperfect. The world is always richer, stranger, and more forgiving than the story we're running about it.

The question is not whether your story is correct. It never fully is. The question is whether you are the kind of person who can notice the miss, hold it honestly, and take the next shot with slightly better information.
